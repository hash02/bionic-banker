From One Script to 14 Agents in 15 Days

I didn't plan to build an autonomous system. I was just trying to check a few transactions.

March 2026 · 12 min read
Case Study — Real-Time Evolution
One engineer. One laptop. 15 days. 14 agents running 24/7.
Every number in this article is real. Every timestamp is verifiable.

The Starting Point Was Embarrassingly Simple

Okay so—this wasn't supposed to be a thing. I'm a computer engineer who works inside finance. I'd been watching how compliance teams handle blockchain transactions and I kept thinking the same thing: there has to be a better way to flag suspicious activity than manually checking addresses against a list.

So I wrote a Python script. One file. Maybe 80 lines. It pulled a few transactions from Etherscan, compared addresses against the OFAC sanctions list, and printed out which ones matched. That was it. That was the whole "product."

That was Day 0. Fifteen days later, I had 14 autonomous agents running on a Dell laptop, generating 438 reports, monitoring 190 blockchain wallets, executing paper trades, writing content drafts, stress-testing their own security, and talking to me through Telegram—all while I was at work.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about building with AI: the hard part isn't the code. The hard part is that every solution reveals three more problems, and those problems are more interesting than what you were originally doing. You follow the dots. One to the next to the next to the next. And you look up and suddenly you've built something you didn't plan to build.

The Evolution—Day by Day

I want to walk through this honestly, because the clean version ("I had a vision and executed it") isn't what happened. What actually happened was messier, more interesting, and a lot more instructive if you're thinking about building something similar.

Days 1–3: The AML Engine

Started with that 80-line script. Then I thought: what if it could check more than just OFAC? What about mixer interactions? High-value transfers? Repeat offenders? Each question became a rule. By Day 3 I had v1 of an AML detection engine with 8 rules and a basic scoring system. Still one script, but now it was doing something genuinely useful—flagging transactions that real compliance teams would want to see.

The key decision here: I didn't wait until it was perfect. I deployed a Streamlit demo on Day 3 and put it on GitHub. The engine was rough, but it was visible. That visibility forced discipline. If anyone could see it, it had to actually work.

Days 4–6: The Blog and the First Content

I realized something: building the engine taught me things that other people would want to know. How OFAC sanctions actually work on-chain. Why Tornado Cash is harder to detect than people think. What compliance teams actually look at versus what Twitter thinks they look at.

So I built bionicbanker.tech. Not a fancy platform—static HTML, dark theme, deployed on Cloudflare Pages. Wrote the first articles about the AML engine, blockchain trends, and what zero-knowledge proofs actually do. The writing forced me to understand my own work better. Feynman was right—if you can't explain it, you don't understand it.

Days 7–8: The Engine Levels Up

The AML engine hit v11. 22 rules. 94.9% detection rate on known sanctioned addresses. It could catch Lazarus Group wallets, Tornado Cash interactions, structuring patterns, repeat offenders. I added a proper scoring algorithm with weighted risk factors. The Streamlit demo went live with interactive charts.

But here's where the interesting turn happened. I had an engine that generated data every time it ran. Reports, risk scores, flagged addresses. That data was just sitting there. What if something else could USE that data?

Days 9–10: Two Brains

I was running two AI sessions simultaneously—one for strategy and content (on my desktop), one for code execution (on a Dell laptop running Ubuntu). And I kept copy-pasting between them. Commands from one machine, results from the other. It was slow and error-prone.

So I built a bridge. SSH connection between the two machines. One brain designs, the other executes. They communicate through markdown files—one writes a task list, the other reads it, does the work, writes back the results. Like a nervous system connecting a mind and a body.

This was the inflection point. The moment I stopped thinking "two tools" and started thinking "one system, two brains," everything accelerated. Because now I could design complex workflows on the desktop and have them execute automatically on the Dell. The coordination wasn't manual anymore—it was structural.

Days 11–13: The Agent Army

Once the two-brain architecture existed, agents became inevitable. If the AML engine generates risk data, something should turn that data into content. If content gets written, something should check if the website is still healthy. If wallets are being monitored, something should track them over time and detect trends.

Each agent does one thing. Not two. One job, one output format, one optimization loop. A wallet monitor that runs every hour. A content scout that reads AML reports and generates article ideas. A sentiment analyzer that tracks market fear and greed. A paper trader that executes DCA strategies based on signals from other agents.

And then there's KALA—the kill agent. KALA's only job is to destroy the system. Every hour, it runs attack scenarios: inject corrupt data, simulate bridge failures, test what happens when agents go silent, check if fake rules can slip through the approval gate. It has a 20% survival rate, meaning 80% of its attacks find something that breaks. Every failure becomes a new rule in the system's immune memory. Every survival means KALA escalates to a harder attack next time. I published those numbers honestly because that's the point—resilience isn't built by hoping nothing breaks, it's built by breaking things intentionally and documenting what happens.

They all write to the same SQLite database. They all communicate through an internal event bus. Any agent can read any other agent's output. The AML monitor's risk data feeds the content scout's ideas. The content scout's drafts feed the social posting pipeline. The paper trader's signals come from the sentiment analyzer. Everything connects.

Days 14–15: Three Brains and Mutual Oversight

I added a third brain. A conversational AI on Telegram that interacts with me like a learning companion—gamified, playful, using the same data all the other agents generate. But more importantly, it created a triangle of mutual oversight.

Here's why that matters: a two-brain system has a single point of failure. If one goes rogue (bad strategy, corrupted code, hallucinated plans), the other has no tiebreaker. Three brains create fault tolerance. Any two can outvote one. If the mind proposes something the body and the face both reject, the proposal dies. It's not hierarchy—it's consensus.

This is the same principle that makes blockchain work. No single node is trusted. The network agrees or the block doesn't get added. Except here, the "nodes" are AI agents with different capabilities and perspectives.

What I Actually Learned

Building this system taught me things that no tutorial or documentation ever mentioned. Here are the ones that stuck:

The output IS the product. I spent the first week focused on building. Then I realized: every report the system generates, every risk score it calculates, every market signal it detects—that's not just data, that's a timestamp-verified proof of capability. Every day the system runs without intervention, it's writing its own track record. The existence IS the pitch.

Agent identity matters more than agent code. When I started giving agents distinct personalities and clear single-purpose missions, their output quality jumped. An agent that "monitors AML stuff" produces mediocre work. An agent that IS a protection agent, with a name and a mission and an identity file that says "I guard this system"—that agent produces focused, consistent output. It's the same principle as writing a good job description: clarity of role creates clarity of output.

The bridge is everything. The hardest engineering wasn't any individual agent. It was the communication layer between them. How do you make sure Agent A's output is available to Agent B? How do you prevent two agents from stepping on each other? How do you know when an agent has silently failed? The plumbing is 80% of the work. The intelligence is 20%.

Break things on purpose. I built a stress-testing agent that runs daily kill scenarios—simulating crashes, injecting bad data, testing what happens when the bridge goes down. Every failure it finds becomes a new rule in the system's immune memory. Every survival makes the system stronger. This is the most counterintuitive thing I built, and it's the most valuable. A system that's never been tested is a system waiting to fail.

Free tools go surprisingly far. The entire system runs on free APIs (DeFiLlama, Blockscout, Crypto.com), local AI models for reasoning, and one paid AI subscription for the heavy thinking. Total ongoing cost: less than $20/month. The constraint of "spend nothing" forced creative architecture. I couldn't throw money at problems, so I had to think through them.

Where This Goes

I'm not going to pretend I have the next five years mapped out. I don't. What I have is a system that generates real data, a website that explains what I've learned, and a track record that grows every day without me touching it.

The AML engine is live and demonstrable. The agent army is running and producing. The content pipeline turns system output into articles and social posts. The paper trading system is building a verifiable track record. And all of it started with 80 lines of Python checking a few blockchain transactions.

If you're thinking about building something similar, here's my honest advice: start with the smallest possible thing that produces a visible output. Not a plan. Not an architecture diagram. A script that does one thing and shows you the result. Then follow the dots. The system will tell you what it needs next, if you're listening.

The dots led me from one script to fourteen agents in fifteen days. I have no idea where they lead next. But the system is running, and I'm paying attention.

"Build the machine that builds the thing, don't build the thing directly. But the machine runs on free energy until it generates its own fuel." — a principle I keep coming back to
