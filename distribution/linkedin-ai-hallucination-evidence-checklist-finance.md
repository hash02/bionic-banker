## Caption

An AI answer is not evidence.

That is the sentence finance teams need before they put AI anywhere near compliance, AML, fraud, audit, onboarding, payments, or customer-risk work.

A hallucination does not always look ridiculous.

Sometimes it looks like:

- a wrong date
- a stale policy
- a fake source
- a real source used badly
- a missing exception
- a confident summary that drops the one detail a reviewer needed

So the review question should not be:

“Does this answer sound right?”

It should be:

“What evidence would let a human trust, challenge, or reject this claim?”

A practical AI evidence checklist:

1. What exact claim did the AI make?
2. What source supports it?
3. Is the source current?
4. Is the source relevant to this case?
5. What would prove the claim false?
6. What action would this answer trigger?
7. Who reviewed or approved it?
8. What trace or audit artifact was saved?

This matters most when AI output becomes decision-adjacent:

- customer risk labels
- AML escalation narratives
- fraud review summaries
- sanctions or KYC notes
- policy interpretations
- tool calls or workflow actions

The AI can help draft the narrative.

It should not silently become the evidence.

For finance teams, hallucination control is not only a model problem. It is a workflow problem:

claim → source → freshness → relevance → falsifier → reviewer → trail

That is the difference between AI-assisted work and AI-shaped guessing.
