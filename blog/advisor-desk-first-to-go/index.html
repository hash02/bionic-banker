If open banking works, the advisor desk goes first.

The Bank of Canada stalled open banking in the same month it published a paper calling Aave a functioning zero-margin lender. Both signals land on one chair. The financial advisor desk.

Open BankingAIBankingFintech

Two signals from the Bank of Canada in the same month. Aave got a formal research paper calling it a functioning non-bank lending system with zero bad debt. Open banking got a press statement saying a 2026 launch would be premature and ill-advised. Most people read those as separate stories. They are not. They are the same story at different speeds.

The first signal says zero-margin finance works. The second signal says regulated rails are late. Both of those aim at one chair inside Canadian banking. The financial advisor desk.

Let me walk through why.

Quick primer on open banking

Open banking means your bank has to let you send your transaction data to another company through a public API if you ask it to. Phase 1 is read access, so apps can see your accounts and move information. Phase 2 is write access, so apps can actually move money on your behalf. The Consumer-Driven Banking Act received Royal Assent on March 26 this year. The regulator that builds the rails was named. Phase 1 was targeted for early 2026 and did not ship. Phase 2 is on the board for mid-2027. Full timing breakdown is in this morning's piece on the open banking stall.

Short version. The law is real. The rails are not yet. The delay is measured in years, not months.

What Aave proved

The Bank of Canada paper on Aave matters because it is the first time a central bank has said in writing that a DeFi protocol is functioning credit infrastructure. Zero bad debt over the observation window. 24/7 operation. Transparent rules. No loan officer. No human underwriter. Margins compressed to almost nothing because the protocol runs on code, not on overhead.

Full breakdown in the piece on the Aave paper from last week. The headline finding that matters here is the margin one. Aave demonstrates that financial intermediation can be run at a spread approaching zero and still sustain itself, because the cost structure on the other side is also near zero.

Margin is the entire reason a banking branch network exists. When margin compresses, the branch network loses its justification one function at a time.

Why the advisor desk is the first chair to clear

Financial advisors do four things in a bank. They explain products. They fill paperwork. They run quarterly check-ins on a portfolio. They answer FAQ. Every one of those four functions lives in the margin the bank earns on the product they sell. Remove the margin and you remove the reason the product needs a human seller.

Now add an open banking agent with read plus write access. The agent sees every account the customer has, across every institution, in real time. The agent can compare products, quote rates, rebalance, and push the customer into a better instrument without a call. The routine layer of the advisor job becomes something a phone does at two in the morning.

This is not replacement by robot. This is replacement by a combination. Open banking gives the agent the data plumbing. Zero-margin products give the agent something cheap to sell. The advisor seat survives only in the part of the job that is judgment-heavy and messy. Complex estate work. Cross-border tax planning. Business succession. Clients under stress making large irreversible decisions. That work does not compress.

The routine 80 percent is what gets cleared first. Which is a lot of desks.

The pincer

If the law did not exist, the tech wave would force the rails anyway. Apps like Revolut, Monzo, Wise, and Plaid already run a version of this in other countries because customer demand does not stop at a regulator's door. Every year the public rail is delayed, private one-off data-sharing deals between big banks and specific fintechs get normalized. That is a private version of open banking without any of the consumer protections a public rail is supposed to provide.

If the tech wave did not exist, the law would force the rails anyway. The Act is on the books. Screen scraping is now an offence. Phase 1 has a regulatory clock on it. The launch can be delayed. The destination cannot be cancelled.

Both arms of the pincer push the same direction. Open banking is arriving. The margin compression that follows is arriving with it.

What an advisor role looks like in 2028

I do not think the advisor disappears. I think the shape of the job shifts and the headcount compresses. A plausible end state. Fewer advisors per branch. Each one covers more clients but only on the judgment layer. The routine rebase, rebalance, quarterly check-in, and product-explanation flow goes to an agent the client talks to through their bank app. The advisor gets pulled in only when the agent flags something non-routine.

Two variables decide how fast this hits. The speed at which open banking rails actually ship. The speed at which bank internal tooling catches up to what fintechs have already deployed overseas. The first is slow. The second is accelerating.

The advisors who survive this shift are the ones who learn to work with the agent layer instead of competing with it. The ones who can operate both sides of the desk. Client-facing judgment plus an understanding of what the agent just recommended and why.

The play if this is your job

Eighteen to twenty-four months is the window. That is roughly the gap between open banking Phase 1 actually shipping and the point where agent-driven product distribution becomes standard inside bank apps. Inside that window, the person who understands both the regulated advisor side and the agent side is worth more than almost anyone on the floor.

Three concrete steps. Learn what an open banking API actually does, read versus write, and what a consent flow looks like. Learn what current agents can and cannot do in a regulated context. Read one piece of actual code that builds an agent, end to end, so the language is not abstract. And pick one judgment-heavy sub-specialty inside advisory that does not compress. Estate. Cross-border. Business succession. Complex tax. Build the deep stack there.

The people who do none of that will be fine for a while. Margin compression is slow at first. Then it is not.

The one thing to remember

The margin is the job. Aave proved the margin can go to zero and the system still works. Open banking proves the rails that let any fintech quote into the Canadian market are on their way. The advisor desk sits downstream of both of those conditions.

Do not wait for a memo. The memo is already public. The Bank of Canada wrote it twice this month and almost nobody read both papers together.
