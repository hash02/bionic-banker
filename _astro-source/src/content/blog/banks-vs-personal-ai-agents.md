By Hash

I learned about money and security in crypto before I ever worked inside a bank. In DeFi, the crypto apps with no bank in the middle, the first rule is blunt: never hand over your key. Whoever holds the key can move everything. If they move it the wrong way, there is no help desk, and nobody gives it back. A mistake I signed was mine to keep.

Then I went into compliance work at a bank, and learned the opposite habit. A person checks. A process keeps the record. When something goes wrong, there is someone to call, and often a guarantee standing behind the customer.

This fall, those two lessons met head on. Banks in Canada and the United States started drawing lines around personal AI agents, the assistants that can sign in to your account and act for you. I read the notices, the terms and the new products on the other side, and I kept asking the two questions those years taught me. Who holds the key? And who pays when it goes wrong?

This guide is my full answer. The short version: an AI can help with your money without ever holding your key, and the safe tools are the ones built so that a person approves before money moves. If you hand an AI your password anyway, the written terms I read put the loss on you.

## What happened this fall

In late October 2026, some of Canada's biggest banks said they would block or limit personal AI agents from reaching client accounts. These are AI assistants that can sign in, read balances and act on a person's behalf. One bank, in a page on its own site dated October 27, 2026, said it would block third party personal AI agents from direct access to its online banking.

The same pattern showed up in the United States. Axios reported on October 22, 2026, that several large US banks were restricting personal AI agents. Chase's own Digital Services Agreement, effective October 27, 2026, prohibits third parties, including "External AI," from using a customer's login credentials, while still allowing approved connections that the customer authorizes.

Then, on November 5, 2026, Wealthsimple announced Penny, an AI assistant that prepares e-Transfers, wires, bill payments and trades, and never moves money or places a trade without the client's confirmation. Penny is announced, not launched; Wealthsimple lists it as coming in early 2027, along with connections to ChatGPT and Claude where clients choose the level of access and critical actions get an extra check.

The headlines made this look like a fight between banks and AI. Read the actual wording and it looks more like one line being drawn, between two kinds of access.

## Two kinds of access

The first kind is the password handover. The agent signs in as you, with your username, password and one time codes. From the bank's side, every click looks like yours. This is the door the restrictions are closing.

The second kind is narrower. The agent connects through a door built for it. You approve a specific scope, such as "read these accounts" or "prepare this payment." The access can end, you can take it back, and when money is about to move, a person still says yes.

Penny is one Canadian example of the second kind. Another is Mercury, a US financial technology platform, where a connected AI tool can request a transfer or payment, but confirming it in the AI chat does not send it. A person has to approve each request inside Mercury before money moves.

## The three questions test

Every AI money tool I looked at could be judged with three questions. I call this the three questions test.

1. Can the bank tell the agent from you?
2. Is the agent's permission limited, and can you take it back?
3. When money moves, does a person approve it first?

Run the password handover through it. The bank cannot tell the agent from you, because it is using your login. The permission is not limited, because your password opens everything you can open. And whether a person approves each payment depends entirely on how the agent was built.

Run Penny, as announced, through it. The agent works inside Wealthsimple's own environment, so it is identifiable. Clients will choose access levels for outside tools. And no money moves without the client confirming. Three clear answers, on paper. Since Penny has not launched, that is a reading of the announcement, not a test of the product.

When the test does not apply: an AI that only reads a summary you typed yourself, with account numbers removed, never touches your account. There is nothing to identify, limit or approve.

## What to do instead of giving an AI your password

1. **Never share your login, one time codes or security answers.** The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security's October 2026 online banking guidance says it in one line: never share your login details, one time codes or security answers with anyone, including third party apps and services.

2. **Connect, don't log in.** Canada's Consumer Driven Banking Act received Royal Assent on March 26, 2026. Its first phase is read only data sharing through secure connections, without handing over your login. About nine million Canadians share financial data through screen scraping today, according to the Department of Finance, a practice it says carries security, privacy and liability risks.

3. **Share only what is needed.** Seeing your balance is not the same as moving your money. Give read access when read access is enough.

4. **Know how to take it back.** The Cyber Centre warns that access can stay on after you stop using an app. Check your linked apps every few months and remove the ones you don't use.

5. **Turn on alerts.** Get a notice for every sign in, payment and account change. The Cyber Centre's rule: only approve sign in or payment requests that you started yourself.

6. **Ask, don't hand over.** Want AI help with a budget? Give it a summary with account numbers removed. It can still do the math.

## The question underneath: who pays when it goes wrong?

Three questions decide whether a tool is built safely. One more decides what happens when it isn't: whose loss is it?

Banks already answer that for ordinary fraud. One Canadian bank's guarantee terms say you are fully protected against eligible digital transactions "you didn't make or approve so long as you fulfill your responsibilities." The same page lists when the bank will not reimburse you. Two items on that list matter here: if "you share any of your Passwords," and if "you consent to, contribute to or authorize a transaction in any way."

Now picture an agent that used your password to send a payment you never meant to send. You shared the password. You let the agent in. Read plainly, that wording points the loss back at you.

Chase's terms say it more directly. Its Digital Services Agreement, effective October 27, 2026, says that if you let a third party, "including External AI," into your accounts, you are deemed to "expressly authorize that third party to transact in your account." You also agree that you are "responsible for any resulting errors, including where the third party performs unintended actions, makes transactions you did not intend, or exceeds the authority you gave it," and that, to the fullest extent permitted by law, you "assume all risks and losses associated with such access or use."

The scoped tools read the same way. Mercury's help page says it "is not responsible for any errors, delays, losses, or issues arising from transactions or actions initiated by any authorized third party." Crypto, where I started, says it plainest. Coinbase's documentation for connecting AI agents says: "You are solely responsible for reviewing and authorizing any trades, transfers, or account changes" made through agent workflows. Its advice is to give the agent a separate portfolio and fund it "with only what you're willing to risk."

Wealthsimple's November 5, 2026 announcement says, under its Wealthsimple Pay feature: "Clients who use passkeys are guaranteed reimbursement for unauthorized transactions." That promise is written for unauthorized transactions. The Logic reported the same day that co-founder Brett Huneycutt pointed to that passkey guarantee if an AI assistant makes a mistake, and that two Wealthsimple product leads did not specify who would be responsible if an AI mistake cost a customer money.

So here is what the written terms say, read together. If you hand over access, the loss points at you. Some terms now name AI directly and say so outright. What I did not find is any term that promises to cover a customer when an agent they let in makes a costly mistake.

That is the real reason the line between the two kinds of access matters. With the password handover, the bank cannot tell your agent from you, so on the bank's records every action looks like yours. With a scoped connection, there is a record of what the agent was allowed to do and who approved the payment. That record is where any answer about the loss will have to start.

How I checked: I read in full the Wealthsimple announcement, The Logic's article, the Canadian bank's guarantee page, Chase's Digital Services Agreement, Mercury's help page, Coinbase's agent documentation, the Cyber Centre guidance, OSFI's Guideline E-23 and the Financial Stability Board's report. The Axios report, the Consumer Driven Banking Act's dates and the Department of Finance's nine million figure I confirmed through search summaries, not the full pages. What is not confirmed: how any bank, platform or court would treat a real loss caused by an AI agent. Terms say what a company intends. How a dispute ends is a separate question.

## For the people on the other side of the desk

If you are an advisor or work at a bank, a client's AI agent may already be in your inbox: a transfer request, a trade instruction or a change of address that the client's assistant drafted or sent. It looks like the client. That does not mean it is the client, or that the client meant every word.

Compliance already teaches the habits that work. Before acting on a request an agent wrote or sent, run five checks.

1. **Is it really the client?** Confirm through a contact already on file, not the channel the request came in on.

2. **Is it in scope?** Ask what the client actually allowed the agent to do. Reading balances is not moving money. If the request goes past that, stop and ask.

3. **Does it fit the client's real situation?** Check it against the know your client file: goals, risk tolerance, time horizon and the usual pattern of activity. An agent can write a perfectly worded request that is wrong for the person. Suitability is still a human job.

4. **Who signs off?** A named person approves before money moves. Not the agent, and not "the system."

5. **Keep the record.** Note what came from the agent, what you checked, who approved it and when. If something goes wrong later, that record is where every answer starts.

The rules around this are already written. OSFI's Guideline E-23 on model risk applies to all federally regulated financial institutions in Canada and takes effect May 1, 2027. It expects institutions to keep an inventory of models that carry meaningful risk, including vendor and third party models, and to govern each one across its lifecycle: design, review, deployment, monitoring and retirement. It names autonomous decision making as one of the challenges AI brings that needs its own handling.

The Financial Stability Board's October 15, 2026 report sets out twelve sound practices for using AI in finance. Number ten is "appropriate and effective human oversight." The report also lists risks specific to AI agents, including unauthorized actions, erroneous actions, data exposure, and the plain fact that a person cannot watch every agent decision in real time as use grows.

For clients, the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security gives the simplest message: never share login details, one time codes or security answers with anyone, including third party apps and services, and only approve sign in or payment requests you started yourself.

For anyone choosing tools, Mercury shows one design pattern: the AI can prepare a payment, but saying yes in the chat does not send it. The person approves inside the platform. That is the three questions test, built into the product.

An AI that holds your key can do anything you can. An AI that asks first can only do what you say yes to.
